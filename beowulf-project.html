Manuscript History

Nowell Codex Background The existence of Beowulf today is nothing short of a miracle. The poem survives in a single volume known as the Nowell Codex (or Cotton MS Vitellius A.xv). While the author remains a mystery, we know the physical book was produced by two different scribes working in a "historical window" between 975 and 1025 AD. The first scribe recorded the first 1,939 lines before the second scribe took over to finish the epic.

The Fire of 1731 October 23, 1731 the library that contained this piece caught on fire. In the early hours, the building went up in flames. The keeper of the manuscripts, Richard Bentley, dressed only in wig and nightgown, rescued the fifth-century Codex Alexandrinus - although the story that he leapt through a window to do so is unlikely. Beowulf survived the heat, but the vellum pages were badly scorched and became incredibly brittle.

Restoration and Preservation For over a century, the manuscript was slowly dying; the charred edges would flake off into "dust" every time a page was turned. In 1786, Grímur Jónsson Thorkelin commissioned transcripts. Because he copied the text before the edges had fully crumbled, he preserved hundreds of words that have since physically vanished. It wasn't until 1845 that the British Museum halted this decay by framing every individual page in protective paper mounts. Efforts were not taken until almost 2000 letters had disappeared from the out edge of the pages.

Thematics & Adaptations

Dragons & Smaug Comparison The battle between the Dragon and Beowulf is the first time in European history of a "fire-breathing" dragon. One of the most notorious Beowulf-esque dragons is Smaug. A similarity can be made in the fact that they are drawn to anger by a thief. In Beowulf, the dragon attacks because of the loss of a single chalice. Tolkien has stated Smaug was based off of Fafnir, but the Fire-Breathing aspect has direct ties to Beowulf's dragon.

Symbolism and Sin Dragons in the European world were often associated with the sins of: Greed, Wrath, and Gluttony. These ties to the "Deadly Sins" are due to the association of serpents in pagan mythology, where the Christian churches attempted to merge the two. Calling the dragon "the wicked harmer" is a clear indication of the association with Satan.

Film Adaptations In the 2007 film adaptation, the Dragon is actually part of Beowulf's family—the son of Beowulf and the mother of Grendel. In Beowulf's Dragon, he has a soft underbelly which is exposed enough for Beowulf to stab him. In The Hobbit movie, Smaug is slain by an exposed scale.

Kingship A "good king" was a king who would share his wealth and riches with his people. The opposite is said of Beowulf's dragon, who hoards all he had, in direct juxtaposition with Beowulf.

Wyrd and Lof Wyrd means fate: the characters believe that a person's end is predetermined. For a hero, greatness is facing one's fate with courage. Lof means immortal fame: immortality could only be achieved through stories told by the living. Every heroic act was a calculated effort to ensure their name lived on in the songs of poets (scops).
